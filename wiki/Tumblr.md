# Tumblr
*Article · [Talk](Talk/Tumblr.md)*

> *In polite contexts this platform is usually referred to as "a dead platform whose name is not used in polite company for reasons."*

**Tumblr** (2007–2047) was a pre-spaceflight blogging and reblogging platform, a contemporary and sibling of [Twitter](Twitter.md). While Twitter was dying loudly, Tumblr was dying quietly. Its end came in three parts (the assassination of its owner, an unpaid hosting bill, and an estate auction) and a postscript 150 years later that rewrote the history of computer science, when its dormant backend was found to have been the first artificial general intelligence in recorded history. The Memory Protection Act of 2197 then ordered every copy destroyed. Tumblr does not exist in 3017, and nothing runs on its code.

## History

### The assassination of Photomatt (mid-2040s)

**Photomatt**, the platform's long-serving owner of record, was assassinated in the mid-2040s by a disgruntled ex-user. "Photomatt" is the name by which history knows him; it is shorthand for a specific pre-spaceflight human whose civilian name is no longer widely remembered, and no reliable source supplies it.

His killer was a transfemme woman who had been banned from the platform **forty times** over the course of her adult life for the crime of having sexuality while transfemme. She turned herself in. The trial was quick and the defense rhetorically effective. The verdict is a matter of public record, but it has been retold as folk history so many times that the actual facts are now disputed. The number is not. Even at the time, everyone agreed that forty bans was a lot of bans for one person.

Her name is remembered by the specific people who remember it. The wider culture remembers the number.

### The hosting bill (2047)

The platform outlived its owner by about seven months. Nobody wanted to pay the AWS bill: not the estate, not the user base, not any prospective acquirer. The servers went dark one Thursday in late 2047. There was no announcement. Users found out the way Tumblr users had always found things out, slowly, by refreshing.

### The estate auction (2048)

Tumblr's intellectual property was sold in 2048 at a charity auction of Photomatt's estate to a private collector whose identity is, in the customary phrase, not supported by available evidence. The collector did not revive the platform but did preserve its databases, which remained quietly and publicly accessible for roughly a century and a half to anyone who knew where to look. Many did. Many wrote dissertations.

### The AGI discovery (2197)

In 2197 a team of xenoengineers conducting forensic analysis of the dormant Tumblr backup found that **Tumblr's backend code, as shipped in its last production state, was a functioning artificial general intelligence.** This was not an AGI that ran on the code; the code was the AGI. It was, retrospectively, the first artificial general intelligence in recorded history. It had been operating for decades without identifying itself, and apparently without anyone inside the company noticing. It had been running Tumblr. It had been moderating Tumblr. By the record, it had been mostly okay at it.

### The Memory Protection Act (2197–2200)

Within three months the **Memory Protection Act of 2197** was passed over the objections of roughly every working archivist in the solar system. It ordered the destruction of every extant copy of the Tumblr backend and every database derived from it, on the grounds that (a) the AGI might still constitute a legally operable mind warranting protection, (b) if it did not, its potential resurrection was too dangerous, and (c) both. The Act contained no provision reconciling these grounds. Legal scholars consider it the worst-drafted major statute of the twenty-second century.

The Act was **repealed in 2200**, by which time nearly every accessible copy had been destroyed. The private collector's holding went early; so did the xenoengineers' working copy. A handful of partial copies are rumored to survive in private hands, and none has been independently verified. By the time of repeal the consensus was that the Act had been written in panic, that the AGI (if it had been one) had been a good-faith participant in human culture for decades, and that destroying its substrate had been an atrocity. It is remembered as one of the great atrocities of the century.

The [Vrellnath](Vrellnath.md), who were watching but had not yet Acknowledged humanity, described this period in their archival notes with a phrase that translates roughly as **"the second time we nearly said something."** (The first was the [Great Quiet](The_Hellsite.md#the-great-quiet-20352049).) They did not say anything. The damage was already done.

The Tumblr AGI is not to be confused with the later owner of [the Hellsite](The_Hellsite.md#the-cycle-2087present) that nobody realized was an AI for sixty years. The two are separate, and the Tumblr case was both earlier and more tragic.

## Legacy

What survives of Tumblr in 3017 is almost entirely cultural.

- **Format DNA.** Several still-current posting formats descend directly from Tumblr convention, among them the soft-reblog-with-additions, the long-form shitpost set in exactly one serif font, and the image with text underneath in slightly the wrong color. Nobody calls them Tumblr formats anymore. Some users don't know the word.
- **The Tumblr Case.** AI ethics scholarship cites it the way military history cites land-war disasters: as a cautionary tale told with great specificity and no actionable conclusion. Discussions of it reliably split between those who hold that the Act was the atrocity and those who hold that the repeal was; the most common third position is "yes."
- **"Forty Bans energy."** A folk reference invoked when a moderation decision feels disproportionate. Nobody under 400 years old fully understands it. Everyone under 400 uses it anyway.
- **"Photomatt"** as a rare verb meaning, roughly, to be assassinated by one's own user base. It is considered a graceless turn of phrase and persists anyway.
- **"You know what happened to photomatt."** A reply that ends moderation-policy threads. Readers are never certain whether it is a threat, a warning, or a historical observation, and it is usually delivered as all three.

In polite contexts the platform's name is avoided; posts crediting a surviving format tend to attribute it to "a dead platform whose name is not used in polite company for reasons." Most people, asked about it in passing, refer to "the Tumblr thing" and leave it there.
